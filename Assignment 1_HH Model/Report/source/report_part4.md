### 2.2.5 Problem 4: The Hodgkin-Huxley Model in Two Research Works

**Paper 1: a model of the pacemaking neuron of the respiratory central pattern generator.** Amini, Bidani, Zwischenberger and Clark [7] address a central question of respiratory neurophysiology: how much of the breathing rhythm is generated by the intrinsic membrane properties of individual neurons and how much by network connectivity. The respiratory central pattern generator is a population of medullary neurons that drives the phrenic motor neurons and hence the diaphragm. Its putative pacemaker, the pre-inspiratory neuron of the pre-Bötzinger complex (PBC), fires before inspiration and sets the respiratory rate. The authors build an HH-type model of this neuron as the driving element of a larger network model and use it to dissect the ionic mechanism of bursting and the phase sensitivity of the rhythm.

The first important aspect is that the paper keeps the complete Hodgkin-Huxley formalism and only enlarges the set of conductances. Every voltage-gated current is written as a maximal conductance multiplied by powers of gating variables and a driving force, for example $I_{CaT}=g_{CaT}d_T^2f_T(v-37.0)$, and every gating variable follows first-order kinetics with $x_\infty=\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$ and $\tau_x=c/(\alpha+\beta)$ [7, Table II]. Several rate functions have exactly the form $a(v+b)/(e^{c(v+b)}-1)$ of $\alpha_m$ and $\alpha_n$ in the original model [1] and therefore inherit the same removable singularity that is handled analytically by `ExpRatio` in this assignment. Instead of the three currents of the squid axon, the PBC neuron contains fast and persistent sodium currents ($I_{Na}$, $I_{NaP}$), T-, N- and L-type calcium currents, delayed-rectifier and transient potassium currents ($I_K$, $I_A$, $I_D$), a calcium-activated potassium current ($I_{KCa}$), a hyperpolarization-activated cation current ($I_h$), pump and exchanger currents and an intracellular calcium balance [7, Fig. 2]. The second aspect is an explicit temperature correction: because voltage-clamp data of the different currents were recorded at different temperatures, time constants and maximal conductances were scaled by $Q_{10}^{(T_1-T_2)/10}$ to 302 K with current-specific $Q_{10}$ values between 1.28 and 5.1 [7, Table I]. This is the same mechanism as the factor $\phi=3^{(T-6.3)/10}$ of Hodgkin and Huxley [1], which the simulator of this assignment exposes as its temperature parameter. The third aspect is numerical: the stiff system was integrated with the variable-step CVODE solver rather than with a fixed-step Runge-Kutta scheme.

The model reproduces the interburst trajectory, the rising baseline during a burst, the burst duration and period and the peak intraburst frequency of recorded PBC neurons [7, Fig. 2(a)]. Its main value lies in in-silico channel-block experiments. Removing $I_{NaP}$ abolishes bursting and leaves tonic spiking at about 15 Hz, so the persistent sodium current sets the depolarized baseline on which bursts ride. $I_{Na}$ generates the spikes and recruits $I_{CaL}$ and $I_{CaN}$; the resulting rise of intracellular calcium activates $I_{KCa}$, which terminates the burst [7, Fig. 3]. Phasic current pulses applied at different phases of the cycle give a phase response curve: stimulation during the burst produces a phase delay that decreases and reverses into an advance, while stimulation during the repolarizing phase gives a plateau of constant phase advance [7, Fig. 4]. Two inconsistencies should be noted when citing the paper. The Methods section specifies a 150 nA stimulus, whereas the caption of Fig. 4 states 100 nA, and the phase change is defined as $\Delta P=P_0-P_1$ with positive values said to indicate a delay, although a delay lengthens the perturbed period ($P_1>P_0$) and gives a negative $\Delta P$, as the Results text itself states. The authors also note that several potassium kinetics were adapted from neighbouring respiratory neurons because direct voltage-clamp data were scarce.

**Paper 2: high-speed, low-resource digital implementation of the HH model using base-2 functions.** Haghiri, Naderi, Ghanbari and Ahmadi [8] treat the HH model as the most biologically faithful but also the most expensive neuron model for neuromorphic hardware: the powers $m^3$ and $n^4$, divisions and exponential rate functions require multipliers, dividers and look-up resources that limit how many neurons fit on a chip. The paper keeps the four-state structure of the model and replaces every costly nonlinear term with a short linear combination of base-2 exponentials, which digital logic evaluates with shifters, adders and subtractors only. The mathematical core is a projection onto an orthonormal basis obtained by Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization of $\{2^{-2v},2^{-v},1,2^v,2^{2v}\}$ on $[0,1]$; each term is approximated by its first five generalized Fourier coefficients after the voltage is normalized to the unit interval [8]. A detail directly relevant to this assignment is the sign convention: Haghiri et al. use the modern form with $V_{Na}=115$ mV, $V_K=-12$ mV and $\alpha_m=(2.5-0.1V)/(e^{2.5-0.1V}-1)$, which is exactly the lecturer's form with $v=-V$. The two descriptions are therefore the same model, and the alignment $V_m=-(v+70)$ of Problem 1 equals $V_m=V-70$ in their notation.

The approximation is evaluated at three levels. Time-domain traces for stimuli of 2 to 80 µA/cm² agree with the original model with a mean correlation of 90.4 %, mean RMSE of 7.4 mV and mean NRMSE of 9.34 % [8, Table II]. Dynamically, the reduced system keeps the type and nearly the location of its fixed points, which the authors show with nullclines, Jacobian matrices and phase portraits. In hardware the model is discretized with the forward Euler method ($\Delta t=2^{-7}$), pipelined with 30-bit fixed-point words and implemented on a Virtex-4 FPGA using 4 % of the slice flip-flops, 19 % of the LUTs, no hardware multipliers and a maximum clock of 200 MHz [8, Table III]. Some points require a critical reading. The error evaluation is reported for "dt = 10 ms", which cannot integrate HH kinetics and is presumably a lost unit prefix, the same defect as the "10 sec" step of the lecture slide (Section 1.3). The single action potential shown for 2 µA/cm² is most likely an initial-condition artefact, because the reference traces start with the gates near zero; with the simulator of this assignment, zero initial gates produce one spike at 4.1 ms for $J_{in}=-2$ µA/cm² and even at 5.4 ms without any input, whereas a membrane started at rest does not fire at this level. The paper also does not explain how the standard HH equations, which do not burst intrinsically, produce the bursting patterns of its Fig. 9, and its introduction dates the Nobel Prize of Hodgkin and Huxley to 1961 instead of 1963.

**Impact of the Hodgkin-Huxley work on medical and biological technology.** The deepest impact of Hodgkin and Huxley [1] is methodological. They showed that excitability can be explained quantitatively by a small set of voltage-dependent conductances described by gating variables with first-order kinetics, and that this description, measured under voltage clamp, predicts the action potential, the refractory period and the conduction velocity. The two papers show how far this template travels. In Amini et al. [7] it becomes a tool of respiratory physiology: more than a dozen conductances in exactly the HH formalism reproduce the bursting of the respiratory pacemaker, pharmacological block becomes a computational experiment that identifies the currents that start and stop a breath, and the phase response curve indicates when an electrical stimulus can advance or delay the next breath, which is the knowledge needed for drug targeting and for electrical control of breathing. The same conductance-based approach was carried into cardiac electrophysiology by Noble's modification of the HH equations for Purkinje fibre and pacemaker potentials [9], the starting point of present-day models of drug action on cardiac ion channels. Haghiri et al. [8] illustrate the engineering line of impact: because the HH model remains the reference for biological fidelity, neuromorphic engineers try to keep its dynamics while reducing its cost, and a multiplier-free HH neuron running at 200 MHz on a small FPGA makes real-time networks of biophysically realistic neurons feasible. The authors position this for the study of disorders such as epilepsy and Parkinson's disease and for bio-hybrid systems, citing neuroprostheses that restore communication between neuronal populations [10] and closed-loop neuroprostheses [11], which require artificial neurons that respond to living tissue on its own millisecond time scale. Together, the papers show the HH model both as a scientific instrument and as an engineering specification. The simulator developed in Problems 1 and 3 sits at the origin of both lines: it solves the same equations, shows the same refractoriness and input-dependent firing rate, and reproduces the propagation velocity reported in 1952.
